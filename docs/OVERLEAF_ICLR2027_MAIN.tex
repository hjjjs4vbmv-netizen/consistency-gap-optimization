\begin{abstract}
Easy Consistency Tuning (ECT) trains one-step generators from states paired at
a temporal separation, but the optimization meaning of that pair spacing is
unclear. We show that changing the spacing is an exact composite intervention:
it moves the detached target endpoint and changes the explicit inverse-gap
weight. These two channels factorize at a matched objective-gradient state,
yet the factorization does not determine outcomes after training arms acquire
different parameters and optimizer histories. We test this distinction in a
fixed-$q=256$ CIFAR-10 ECT configuration using a controlled $g=1.10$ spacing
probe. A three-seed formal factorial favored the complete intervention over the
baseline in all 12 seedwise FID/KID directions across one- and two-step
sampling at 256 kimg. Audited exact-budget replays and two secondary cohorts
then track 456 paired FID/KID evaluation jobs through 1024 kimg. At the descriptive
one-step FID threshold of 10, the complete intervention reached the threshold
one 128-kimg stage earlier for five of ten training seeds; two seeds tied, two
crossed later, and one pair was censored. Four-arm rankings also changed with
seed, budget, and sampling NFE. Pair spacing can therefore advance entry into
a useful finite-budget regime, but the advance is not universal. In this
setting, pair spacing is best understood as a locally structured training
control with trajectory- and horizon-dependent consequences, not as a
universally superior final hyperparameter.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Consistency models replace iterative diffusion sampling with a mapping that is
constant along a probability-flow trajectory, enabling one- or few-step
generation \citep{song2023consistency}. Their sampling speed shifts part of the
computational burden to training: a useful model must learn a long trajectory
relation from local or discretized consistency constraints. Improved
consistency training and Easy Consistency Tuning (ECT) make this process far
more effective \citep{song2024improved,geng2024ect}, but they also introduce a
concrete control question. How far apart should the two training states be?
This question matters at finite budget because pair spacing can change when a
one-step model first enters a useful-quality regime, even if differences later
contract.

Pair spacing is easy to misread as a scalar knob. In the ECT objective studied
here, a larger spacing changes which earlier state supplies the detached target
and also changes the explicit inverse-gap weight multiplying the residual.
These are distinct interventions. At one matched model state, their action on
the loss and one-sided training derivative is exactly characterizable. After a
single update, however, separately trained arms generally no longer share
parameters, stochastic gradients, or optimizer state. A local identity is
therefore not an endpoint-ranking theorem. This local-to-trajectory distinction
is the organizing problem of the paper (Figure~\ref{fig:composite}).

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{figures/figure1_composite_intervention.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Pair spacing is a composite local intervention.} In the
  studied ECT objective, changing $\Delta$ moves the detached target endpoint
  and changes the explicit inverse-gap weight. Their matched-state
  loss/gradient action factorizes exactly. Separate finite training then
  creates different parameter and optimizer histories, so time-to-quality and
  arm ranking remain empirical, horizon-dependent quantities. The fixed
  $g=1.10$ multiplier is a controlled probe, not a proposed universal method.}
  \label{fig:composite}
\end{figure*}

We study this problem at fixed $q=256$. Both $q$ and the multiplier $g$ can
affect realized pair spacing; our experiment changes only $g$ and does not
establish invariance across $q$. The novelty is consequently not that
$g=1.10$ is ``better.'' It is the conjunction of (i) an exact ECT-specific
composite-intervention formulation, (ii) a target-by-denominator factorial
followed through complete learning trajectories, and (iii) a seed-resolved
account of when the intervention advances, ties, or delays entry into a
high-quality one-step regime.

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We derive exact finite-gap loss and one-sided gradient decompositions,
  exact target-by-denominator identities, and a conditional local scaling law
  $\kappa=\nu(p-1)-\alpha$ that specializes to $\kappa=-1$ for the ECT loss.
  \item We combine a formal three-seed factorial with audited deterministic
  four-arm replays over seven budgets. This design separates objective-level
  factorization from finite-training outcome non-factorization.
  \item We quantify finite-budget phase advance without averaging away adverse
  seeds. Across three evidence groups, the complete intervention is one
  observed stage earlier for five seeds, tied for two, later for two, and
  censored for one; component rankings also depend on horizon and sampling NFE.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work and Problem Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Training fast generative models}

Consistency models can be trained by distillation or directly from data
\citep{song2023consistency}. Improved consistency training (iCT) corrected the
teacher update and introduced loss, noise-sampling, and discretization choices
that substantially strengthened training from scratch
\citep{song2024improved}. ECT instead fine-tunes a pretrained diffusion model
while progressively strengthening consistency constraints
\citep{geng2024ect}. Related work improves continuous-time parameterization and
stability \citep{lu2025scm}, reduces target variance
\citep{wang2024sct,silvestri2025vct}, or adapts traversal difficulty and
discretization \citep{liu2025ccm,bai2025adcm}. These methods establish that
pair construction, weighting, and trajectory coverage affect trainability.
Our question is narrower: for one fixed ECT configuration, what intervention
does pair spacing implement locally, and how does that intervention appear
along finite training trajectories?

Adaptive optimizers provide a relevant boundary. Adam and RAdam retain unequal
moment histories \citep{kingma2015adam,liu2020radam}, and nonstationary gradient
magnitude can interact with stored moments \citep{ellis2024adamrel}. Gradient
scaling therefore need not imply trajectory equivalence. Section~\ref{sec:optimizer}
uses a frozen-state ECT diagnostic only to establish this boundary.

\subsection{ECT objective and factorial arms}

For a coupled sample, write $x_t=y+t\epsilon$ and $x_r=y+r\epsilon$, with
$\Delta=t-r>0$. The online output is $D_t=f_\theta(x_t,t)$ and the target is
$T_r=\sg[f_\theta(x_r,r)]$. The studied loss uses the Euclidean residual and
an external inverse-gap weight,
\begin{equation}
  \ell(r,\Delta)=\frac{\lVert D_t-T_r\rVert_2}{\Delta}.
  \label{eq:ect-loss}
\end{equation}
We compare the native baseline spacing $\Delta_1$ with the realized spacing
$\Delta_g$ under $g=1.10$, including the production boundary clamp. The four
training arms cross the detached target and denominator assignments:

\section{Exact Local Structure of the Intervention}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Ideal endpoint invariance is conditional}

Pair spacing can change training without changing an ideal consistency map.
Let $\mathcal{T}_{t\rightarrow r}$ be exact compositional transport along a
trajectory. Suppose a schedule connects every supported $t$ to a common
anchor $t_\star$, every scheduled edge is covered by the population objective,
the function class attains zero edge residual, optimization reaches that
solution, and the map is fixed at the anchor. Zero residual then telescopes:
\begin{equation}
  F^\star(x_t,t)=F^\star(\mathcal{T}_{t\rightarrow t_\star}(x_t),t_\star).
  \label{eq:ideal-invariance}
\end{equation}
Two legal schedules with the same support, transport, and anchor therefore
identify the same ideal map on reachable states. This is an identification
statement under strong assumptions, not a claim about finite-capacity or
incompletely optimized networks \citep{lyu2024convergence,dou2024theory}.

\subsection{One-sided derivative and exact finite-gap decomposition}

Stop-gradient must be represented at the derivative level. Introduce a lifted
loss $\widetilde\ell(\theta,\bar\theta)$ whose target branch uses
$\bar\theta$. The training derivative is
\begin{equation}
  \Dsg\ell:=\left.\partial_\theta
  \widetilde\ell(\theta,\bar\theta)\right|_{\bar\theta=\theta},
  \label{eq:sg-derivative}
\end{equation}
not the diagonal total derivative, which would also differentiate the target
copy. Let $e_r=D_t-T_r$, $u_r=e_r/\lVert e_r\rVert_2$, and
$J_t=\partial_\theta D_t$. Away from $e_r=0$, the implemented per-sample
training derivative is exactly
\begin{equation}
  g(r,\Delta):=\Dsg\ell(r,\Delta)=\frac{1}{\Delta}J_t^\top u_r.
  \label{eq:exact-gradient}
\end{equation}

Evaluate the baseline and enlarged spacing at the same parameters, online
realization, data, and noise. With $s=\Delta_1/\Delta_g$, substitution into
Equations~\eqref{eq:ect-loss}--\eqref{eq:exact-gradient} yields
\begin{align}
  \ell_g
  &=s\ell_1+\frac{\lVert e_{r_g}\rVert_2-\lVert e_{r_1}\rVert_2}{\Delta_g},
  \label{eq:loss-decomp}\\
  g_g
  &=s g_1+\frac{1}{\Delta_g}J_t^\top(u_{r_g}-u_{r_1}).
  \label{eq:grad-decomp}
\end{align}
The first term is exact denominator rescaling; the second contains the entire
target-endpoint change. No small-gap or optimizer approximation is used.

The factorial follows immediately. At a matched sample,
\begin{equation}
  g_B=s g_C,\qquad g_D=s g_A,\qquad
  g_B-g_D=s(g_C-g_A),
  \label{eq:factorial-identities}
\end{equation}
and the interaction satisfies
\begin{equation}
  g_B-g_C-g_D+g_A=(s-1)(g_C-g_A).
  \label{eq:interaction-identity}
\end{equation}
When the multiplier is unclipped, $s=1/g$ is common and these identities also
hold after minibatch summation. The production manipulation test verified the
two scalar identities with maximum elementwise relative residuals below
$3.9\times10^{-7}$. These are local objective-gradient identities; they neither
rank trained arms nor decompose FID into causal percentages.

\subsection{A conditional local scaling law}

The exact decomposition has a useful asymptotic corollary. Consider
$\ell_\delta=w(\delta,z)\Phi(e_\delta)$ and assume
$e_\delta=\delta^\nu v+O(\delta^{\nu+1})$ with $v\neq0$,
$w(\delta,z)=w_0(z)\delta^{-\alpha}(1+O(\delta))$, a regular online Jacobian,
a nonzero leading derivative, and a degree-$p$ homogeneous $\Phi$. Then
\begin{equation}
  g^{\mathrm{sg}}_\delta=C(\theta,z)\delta^\kappa
  +O(\delta^{\kappa+1}),\qquad
  \boxed{\kappa=\nu(p-1)-\alpha}.
  \label{eq:kappa-law}
\end{equation}
For the present $c=0$ ECT loss, $p=1$ and $\alpha=1$, hence
$\kappa_{\mathrm{ECT}}=-1$. This special case predicts a leading inverse-gap
scale, independent of $\nu$; it therefore does not validate the residual order
$\nu$. It is also local. The finite target correction in
Equation~\eqref{eq:grad-decomp}, minibatch heterogeneity, and all subsequent
optimizer states remain outside the scalar prediction. Appendix~\ref{app:proof}
gives the proof and scope.

\section{Experimental Design and Evidence Status}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Training and evaluation.}
We fine-tuned the same unconditional CIFAR-10 $32\times32$ DDPM++ model with
ECT preconditioning in every arm. Runs used $q=256$, RAdam with learning rate
$10^{-4}$ and $(\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0.9,0.999)$, global batch 128, dropout 0.2,
EMA decay 0.9993, FP16 networks with AMP, and no augmentation or weight decay.
The formal arms began from the same immutable pretrained checkpoint within
each seed and differed only in the target/denominator assignment in
Table~\ref{tab:arms}. FID and KID use 50,000 generated samples, generation
seeds 0--49,999, metric seed 20260730, and FP32 sampling
\citep{heusel2017fid,binkowski2018kid}. NFE2 uses the frozen intermediate time
$0.821$. Training seed is the independent unit; budgets, NFE, and the two
metrics are repeated readouts.

\paragraph{Evidence groups.}
The original formal experiment contains seeds 3--5, four arms, and the
256-kimg endpoint. A deterministic replay of the same 12 trajectories provides
exact checkpoints at 256, 384, 512, 640, 768, 896, and 1024 kimg; all 168
FID/KID jobs passed, and all 12 terminal replay states were bitwise identical
to their canonical 1024-kimg states. Secondary evidence is reported separately:
seeds 6--7 contain A/B curves from 384 to 1024 kimg (48 jobs), and seeds
14--18 contain four-arm curves over the same six budgets (240 jobs). These
secondary cohorts improve descriptive coverage but are not prospectively
relabelled confirmation.

\paragraph{Time-to-quality.}
For arm $a$, seed $s$, and descriptive threshold $\eta=10$, define
\begin{equation}
  \tau_{a,s}(\eta)=\min\{K:\mathrm{FID}_{a,s}^{\mathrm{NFE1}}(K)\leq\eta\}.
  \label{eq:ttq}
\end{equation}
We use observed checkpoints only and report censoring when a crossing is absent.
The threshold summarizes entry into the quality range reached late in training;
it was not preregistered as a confirmatory endpoint. Matched-budget differences
are reported alongside it so the conclusion does not rest on one threshold.

The factorial qualifies the interpretation. At NFE1, both target-related

\subsection{Exact-budget curves reveal contraction and rank changes}

The deterministic replay replaces a single endpoint with the complete formal
learning paths (Figure~\ref{fig:learning-curves}). Early separations can be
large: for example, the NFE1 B$-$A FID difference at 384 kimg is $-65.65$ for

after 640 kimg. Seed 4 changes in the other direction: B is favorable through
512 kimg and slightly unfavorable thereafter. Thus even within one seed,
matched-budget direction is not fixed by the early endpoint.

By 1024 kimg, the formal NFE1 B$-$A differences are $-0.983$, $+0.229$, and

contraction therefore does not produce a universal terminal winner.

\subsection{Phase advance occurs on several, not all, trajectories}

Figure~\ref{fig:ttq} reports the first observed NFE1 checkpoint with FID at
most 10. B crosses one 128-kimg stage earlier for seeds 3, 5, 6, 7, and 14.
Seeds 4 and 15 tie; seeds 17 and 18 cross one stage later under B. Neither arm
crosses for seed 16 by 1024 kimg (A: 13.13; B: 10.92), so that pair is
censored. The phase advance is consequently real on multiple trajectories but
not a seed-uniform property.

Matched-budget comparisons tell the same, less threshold-dependent story. B
has lower NFE1 FID at 16/21 formal replay points, 9/12 secondary A/B points,
and 20/30 secondary factorial points. These are repeated observations along
ten trajectories, not 45 independent wins. At 1024 kimg the favorable B$-$A
counts are 2/3, 2/2, and 3/5 in the three evidence groups, respectively. The
effect therefore persists into a mature-quality regime for several seeds, but
its direction is neither universal nor constant over horizon.

\subsection{Component rankings are horizon- and sampler-dependent}

The formal contrast paths make outcome non-factorization explicit
(Figure~\ref{fig:contrasts}). At 256 kimg, both NFE1 target contrasts are
negative for all three seeds. At 1024 kimg, C$-$A is positive for seeds 3 and 4
but negative for seed 5; denominator and interaction contrasts also mix signs.
The exact relation in Equation~\eqref{eq:interaction-identity} still holds for
a matched objective state, but it does not constrain these separately trained
FID contrasts.

The five-seed secondary factorial supplies an adverse-sensitivity check. At

winners are D, A, D, A, and A. Thus the sampler changes the winner for two of
five seeds, while no single arm wins across the cohort. Early rankings differ
again. The empirical conclusion is therefore narrower than a component
hierarchy: the intervention factorizes exactly at the objective level, while
the functional ranking of its components depends on budget, seed, and sampler.

\section{Supporting Optimizer-State Boundary}
\label{sec:optimizer}

Stateful optimization is one reason a local scalar relation need not become a
trajectory equivalence. We compared virtual RAdam updates after best scalar
alignment,
\begin{equation}
  \Ropt(U_1,U_0)=\frac{\lVert U_1-c^\star U_0\rVert_2}{\lVert U_0\rVert_2},
  \quad c^\star=\frac{\langle U_1,U_0\rangle}{\lVert U_0\rVert_2^2}.
  \label{eq:ropt}
\end{equation}
Across seeds 3--5, the median residual was 0.074--0.088 for observed gradients
under real accumulated state and 0.467--0.491 after moment reset. Even an
exact-scalar gradient pair retained 0.049--0.077 under real state. Moment reset
is therefore not a memory-neutral control. A preregistered moment-transport
attempt subsequently worsened all 12 held-out residuals and failed its gate;
no continuation training or FID/KID evaluation was launched. These diagnostics
support only the boundary that instantaneous gradients do not determine
stateful updates. They do not establish that RAdam caused the quality changes.

\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Scientific interpretation.}
Pair spacing in ECT is locally structured but globally trajectory-dependent.
The exact decomposition explains why the intervention cannot be reduced to a
single unspecified ``gap effect.'' The curves explain why the decomposition
should not be promoted to a fixed quality attribution: each arm changes the
future states at which later objectives are evaluated. The useful practical
quantity is therefore not only terminal FID, but the path from compute to
quality. On five trajectories, the complete probe advances the first observed
high-quality NFE1 checkpoint by one stage. Ties, delays, censoring, and changing
four-arm winners establish the boundary of that advance.

\paragraph{Limitations and next tests.}
The evidence comes from CIFAR-10, one unconditional DDPM++/ECT configuration,
fixed $q=256$, and one spacing multiplier. Both $g$ and $q$ affect effective
pair spacing, so transfer across $q$ is unknown. NFE1 and NFE2 can rank arms
differently, the time-to-quality threshold is descriptive, and the extended
cohorts are secondary/post-preregistration analyses. The optimizer diagnostic
does not close a causal path to FID, and ideal endpoint invariance depends on
the assumptions preceding Equation~\eqref{eq:ideal-invariance}. The highest
priority validation is an effective-spacing-matched cross-$q$ control,
followed by a fresh prospective phase-advance test and a second weighting rule
or dataset. Those tests would determine whether trajectory-conditioned
scheduling is warranted; simply adding more retrospective $q=256$ seeds would
not resolve the main generalization question.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
Changing ECT pair spacing exactly combines a detached-target intervention with
inverse-gap reweighting. This local factorization coexists with finite-budget
learning paths whose quality and component rankings depend on seed, horizon,
and sampler. Pair spacing can advance time-to-quality, but it does not define a
universally best static gap. The resulting view is a bounded one: pair spacing
is a finite-budget training control whose local action can be derived and whose
global consequence must be measured along the trajectory.

\section{Proof and Scope of the Local Scaling Law}
\label{app:proof}

For the lifted one-sided objective, the exact chain rule is
$g^{\mathrm{sg}}_\delta=w(\delta,z){J^+_\delta}^{\!\top}
\nabla\Phi(e_\delta)$. Under the assumptions in Section~\ref{sec:theory},
positive homogeneity gives
\begin{equation}
  \nabla\Phi(e_\delta)=\delta^{\nu(p-1)}
  \left[\nabla\Phi(v)+O(\delta)\right].
\end{equation}
Multiplying by $w_0\delta^{-\alpha}[1+O(\delta)]$ and
${J^+_\delta}^{\!\top}={J^+_0}^{\!\top}+O(\delta)$ proves
Equation~\eqref{eq:kappa-law}, provided
${J^+_0}^{\!\top}\nabla\Phi(v)\neq0$. The theorem does not cover the ordinary
diagonal total derivative, large gaps, a Pseudo-Huber crossover, a
non-smooth clipping boundary, or a degenerate leading term. The exact
finite-gap identities in Equations~\eqref{eq:grad-decomp}--
\eqref{eq:interaction-identity} do not require this asymptotic theorem.
